## 1.1 Playing techniques

| Technique | What the player does | Sound / physical consequence | Game-relevant hooks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Power chord** [M] | Root + fifth (+ octave), usually on the low strings, played with two or three fingers; no third, so it is neither major nor minor | Under heavy distortion, thirds make mud and fifths stay tight; the chord is "neutral" and moves as one shape | Moves in parallel up and down the neck; a three-note shape that is easy to slide; "root + fifth" is an obvious two-part structure |
| **Palm muting** [M] | Right-hand heel rests on the strings near the bridge | Shortens sustain, giving a tight "chug"; low-mid thump | Staccato rhythm; "choked" vs "open" notes: a damped hit is short and heavy, an open hit rings out |
| **Hammer-on / pull-off** [M] | Fretting finger slams onto, or flicks off, a string already ringing, so no pick attack is needed | Fast legato runs with softer attack; the second note is produced by the finger alone | Free extra notes between picks; chaining several in a row |
| **Tapping** [M] | Picking-hand finger hammers onto the fretboard (Eddie Van Halen, "Eruption", 1978) | Lets one hand cover huge intervals fast; the sound is liquid and arpeggiated | Using the other hand; ripple-like repeats |
| **Sweep picking** [M] | One continuous pick stroke across adjacent strings, one note per string | Arpeggios that cascade rapidly; economy of motion | Down-sweep and up-sweep; a "sweep" is a literal arc |
| **Slide** [M] | Finger or glass/metal slide glides between pitches | Continuous pitch change; the "gliss" is vocal | Sliding power chord shapes up and down the neck (also a known rock riff move) |
| **Bend** [M] | Push or pull the string sideways to raise pitch by a half step, whole step or more | The string tension rises, pitch with it; a pre-bend starts already raised | Amount of bend maps to a magnitude; pre-bend, bend-and-release |
| **Vibrato** [M] | Small oscillation of pitch on a held note (wrist vibrato or finger vibrato) | Sustained note "sings" | Rate and width are two dials; a held note that wobbles |
| **Whammy / tremolo-bar dive** [M] | Pressing the bridge bar lowers string tension and pitch; "dive bomb" drops pitch dramatically; the locking Floyd Rose (late 1970s, Floyd Rose) keeps the guitar in tune after abuse [?] date not searched | Pitch scoops, flutters, screams | A drop that recovers to pitch; the risk is going out of tune |
| **Natural harmonic** [V] | Lightly touch the string above the fretwire without pressing. 12th fret = node at half the string = an octave above the open string; 7th (and 19th) = a fifth above that octave; 5th (and 17th) = two octaves up. 7th/5th are harder and need a lighter touch | Bell-like chimes | Frets 12 / 7 / 5 as three fixed "bells"; nodal points; touch-don't-press |
| **Pinch (artificial) harmonic** [M] | Pick hand's thumb brushes the string right after the pick strikes; a high squeal. Associated with Billy Gibbons, Zakk Wylde, Dimebag Darrell [M] | A scream that cuts through distortion | A "squeal" accent on a heavy riff; needs good timing |
| **Tremolo picking** [M] | Very fast alternating picking on one note, 16th notes or faster | A buzzing sustained note that builds tension, e.g. metal and surf guitar | Rate escalation; a sound that feels like a machine gun |
| **Feedback** [M] | Guitar near a loud amp: the pickups catch the amp's sound, which re-vibrates the strings, a loop. Pitch is set by the string/pickup/amp position and the note being fretted. Hendrix is the classic exponent | A sustained howl that sustains forever, rising as you approach the amp, and it can be tuned by moving | A self-feeding loop; gain grows if you stay near; ends when you move away. Cartoon sense: sound waves a toy-sized hero stands beside |

Facts verified by search: natural harmonic fret positions [V]. All others are common knowledge [M].

## 1.2 Gear

- **Amps** [M]: head + cabinet or a combo; tube vs solid-state; clean channel vs gain channel; "turn it up to 11" (This Is Spinal Tap, 1984) is the cultural joke; Marshall stacks = the wall of amps; a Fender "twin" is the clean reference. Volume, gain, bass/mid/treble, presence are the knobs. In the toy-world, an amp is a very large speaker box that can be terrain.
- **Distortion / overdrive / fuzz** [V partially]: Fuzz Face is a fuzz, Tube Screamer is an overdrive; overdrives are the most natural-sounding, a Tube Screamer has a mid-hump EQ and symmetrical clipping, the Boss SD-1 asymmetrical clipping; fuzz is "gnarly, expressive and responds to player dynamics"; a common trick is a fuzz into a Tube Screamer to tighten and compress it [V, guitarworld/pmtonline]. Big Muff is another classic fuzz [V name only]. Mechanism in plain words: the pedal clips the top off the waveform, so quiet notes get loud and the sound gets sustain and harmonics. Distortion = harder clipping than overdrive.
- **Signal-chain order** [V]: Wah first (or after drive as a "parked filter"); octave/pitch early, before drive; drive in the middle; modulation (chorus, flanger, phaser, tremolo) after drive; delay and reverb last; looper at the very end so it records everything. Fuzz can be moved earlier for lead. These are conventions, not rules.
- **Delay** [M]: repeats the signal; time (ms) and feedback (number of repeats) and mix; tap tempo syncs repeats to the beat (dotted-eighth delay is the classic U2/Edge sound). Analog delay repeats darken; tape echo wobbles.
- **Reverb** [M]: spring (Fender amps, "surf" sound), plate, hall, room; "shimmer" reverb adds an octave up to the tail. Long tails smear fast playing.
- **Wah** [M]: a rocker pedal sweeping a band-pass filter; heel down = bassy, toe down = bright "wah"; rhythmic wah = "shaft" funk; Hendrix "Voodoo Child".
- **Looper** [M]: record a phrase, then play over it; overdub layers; stop/undo; Ed Sheeran-style one-man-band. Core mechanic: today's phrase becomes tomorrow's backing.
- **Octave pedal** [M]: adds a signal one octave down (or up); a Digitech Whammy/pitch shifter bends pitch in real time, up to two octaves. Octave-down makes a guitar sound like a bass.
- **Chorus** [M]: copies the signal, detunes and delays it slightly, modulated, giving a shimmering doubled sound; Boss CE-1/CE-2 classics; Nirvana "Come As You Are".
- **Pickups** [M]: single-coil = bright, twangy, noisy (50/60 Hz hum); humbucker = two coils wired out of phase to cancel hum, thick and warm, better with high gain; P-90 in between. Neck pickup warmer, bridge brighter. Active EMG pickups are common in metal. Pickup switch positions give variety.
- **Cables** [M]: a ¼" TS instrument cable; long cables lose treble (capacitance); coiled cable; wireless systems; a tripped-over cable is a classic stage hazard. In game terms: the cable is the tether between guitar and amp and can be terrain or a whip.
- **Picks** [M]: gauge (thin = strum, thick = lead), shape (Jazz III small and pointed; triangle), material (celluloid, nylon, Tortex), also coins and fingernails; the pick is small and gets lost, a natural throwable in a toy-size world.
- **Strings & tuning** [M]: standard tuning E A D G B E; drop D (low E down to D) makes a one-finger power chord, a very metal-friendly mechanic; heavy strings for low tunings.

## 1.3 Music theory that could become mechanics

- **Intervals** [M] (semitones): minor 2nd = 1, major 2nd = 2, minor 3rd = 3, major 3rd = 4, perfect 4th = 5, tritone = 6 ("devil's interval", the diminished fifth), perfect 5th = 7, octave = 12. A power chord is root + 5th. Intervals give consonance/dissonance: 5th and octave sound stable, tritone and minor 2nd sound tense.
- **Chords** [M]: major = root + major 3rd + 5th (bright), minor = root + minor 3rd + 5th (dark), power = root + 5th (neutral), diminished (tense), sus2/sus4 (unresolved), seventh chords (bluesy, dominant 7 wants to resolve). A chord with three notes naturally has three parts.
- **Keys and scales** [M]: major vs minor; pentatonic minor (5 notes) is the basic rock lead scale; the blues scale adds a flat 5th ("blue note"). Playing within the key never sounds wrong. A key is a rule set; a modulation (key change) is a dramatic lift.
- **Chord progressions** [M]: I–IV–V is the 12-bar blues backbone; I–V–vi–IV is the pop-punk staple; tension (V) → release (I) is the main emotional engine: "resolve" = payoff.
- **Tempo and time signatures** [M]: BPM sets speed; 4/4 is the default; 3/4 waltz; 6/8 ballad shuffle; odd meters (5/4 "Take Five"; 7/8 Pink Floyd "Money"). Half-time and double-time feel. Syncopation = accents off the beat. Metronome clicks are the setting's tempo anchor.
- **Call-and-response** [M]: one phrase answered by another; a blues staple, and a duel between guitarists (Dueling Banjos is the folk cousin). Echo/delay pedals do the "response" mechanically.
- **Solos** [M]: a guitar solo is a showcase moment over a backing track; often builds in register and rhythmic density, ending in a held bend or a high note; "shred" = speed, "feel" = expression.
- **Drops** [M]: electronic term for the beat that returns after a build; in rock the equivalent is the breakdown or the chorus entrance after a quiet verse (quiet–loud–quiet, "Smells Like Teen Spirit"). Build → hit → release is a three-beat shape.
- **Dynamics and silence** [M]: loud vs soft; the "stop" before the last chorus; rests matter as much as notes.

## 1.4 Rock culture

- **Stage dive** [M]: jumping from the stage into the crowd, which catches and passes you along (crowd surfing); punk and hardcore origin; Iggy Pop is often credited as an early practitioner [?].
- **Windmill** [V partially]: Pete Townshend's big circular strum; Hendrix is said to have borrowed the hand motion [V]. Townshend's first guitar smash is listed among Rolling Stone's 2004 "50 Moments That Changed Rock & Roll" [V].
- **Smashing guitars** [V]: The Who smashed instruments at Monterey Pop (June 18, 1967); Hendrix then set his Strat on fire later that day, partly trying to top them, though one reviewer sees it as a ritual gift to the crowd. Hendrix had already burned a guitar in London on March 31, 1967 and burned his hands. The Monterey Strat sold at auction in November 2012 for £237,000; whether the burned guitar is the one sold is disputed [V].
- **Encores** [M]: the band leaves, the crowd chants and stomps, the band returns for one or two more songs; often the biggest hit is saved for it. Lighters/phone lights wave during a ballad.
- **Battle of the bands** [M]: local bands compete in short sets; judged by crowd noise or judges; school talent shows and garage-band contests are the setting's common stage.
- **Other tropes** [M]: roadies; backline; "one, two, three, four!" count-in; sound check ("check one-two"); amps stacked in a wall; pyrotechnics; the set list; the drummer's stick click; the tour van; the groupie-free family version is a fan club; mosh pit; devil horns; headbanging; the guitar face; guitar pick tossed to the crowd; the unplugged moment; the feedback squeal at the start or end of a song; tuning between songs; broken-string emergency.

## 2.1 Structure

- **PoE2** [V]: skills are gems, and any class can use any skill; each skill gem has tags (Attack, Spell, Projectile, Melee, Fire, etc.) that determine which modifiers can enhance it. Support gems are socketed directly into skill gems (not a separate link group). Spirit is a reservation resource for auras, minions and Meta gems; Meta gems trigger linked skills (example given: Cast on Shock builds energy when you shock a foe). A Meta gem accepts only spells in its slot per an older forum post [V but possibly outdated]. One guide counts 240 active skill gems and 200 support gems (patch 0.5, updated May 2026) [V, secondary].
- **Diablo 4** [V]: a skill tree arranged in tiers/clusters; Basic skills are generators, usually the weakest attacks, which build the class resource; Core skills are the main damage source and spend it; Ultimate skills have the longest cooldowns, and only one can be equipped. Class resources: Barbarian Fury, Necromancer Essence, Druid Spirit. Barbarian clusters: Basic, Core, Defensive, Brawling, Weapon Mastery, Ultimate, Key Passives [V]. Whirlwind (Barbarian) and Rapid Fire (Rogue) are cited as core skills [V]. Sorcerer and Rogue cluster names were not confirmed [V gap].

## 2.2 How melee, ranged and caster differ in feel and scaling [M unless noted]

| | Melee | Ranged (bow / projectile) | Caster (spell) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feel | Close, heavy, hit-stop and screen shake matter; you take hits and trade; animation commitment is the risk | Kiting, positioning, line-of-sight; many small hits; clearing at distance; projectile speed and count are the "feel" levers | Area shapes, cast time, delays between cast and payoff; mana/resource management; big readable AoE shapes |
| Damage scaling | Weapon damage (flat + %), attack speed, crit, "more" multipliers from skill and supports; melee splash/range | Weapon damage, attack speed, projectile count (fork/chain/pierce/split), projectile damage | Spell damage (no weapon), cast speed, area, element-specific increases, duration for DoTs |
| Defensive coupling | Needs armour/life/block; leech/life-on-hit; fortify-like buffs | Needs evasion/mobility; range is itself defence | Needs energy shield/barrier or distance; CC/slows as defence |
| Typical risks | Getting surrounded; boss telegraphs | Running out of room; low per-hit damage | Cast interruption; resource drain |
| Build identity | Single big hit, or spin/whirlwind clear | Pierce/chain/ricochet clear, charged shots for bosses | Conversion between elements, DoTs, hit vs ailments, chain reactions |

## 2.3 Generators and spenders [M, structure confirmed V for D4]

- Generator (basic) skills have no cost or are cheap and give a resource per hit; spenders cost the resource and hit much harder or in larger areas. The fun loop is "build, build, build, spend": a rhythm with a clear beat. Variants: resource decays out of combat (Rogue energy-style), builder skills gain extra effects with upgrades, spenders can refund on kill.
- In PoE2 there is no single class resource; instead skills have mana costs, cooldowns, charges, or combo mechanics (e.g., attacks that build "Combo" or "Gems with a buildup") [M, unverified].

## 2.4 Support gems [V partially, M mostly]

- Support gems modify an active skill in a bounded, readable way: add +X% more damage with a downside (cost, longer cast), add chain/fork/pierce, convert damage type, add an ailment, trigger on an event (meta). Because supports are chosen, builds diverge from one skill. The skill's tags decide which supports are allowed.
- Design lesson: each skill has 3–5 "natural" supports that rewire it without breaking its one clear idea.

## 1. melee | standing waves and resonance in bridges | roguelike deckbuilder

**Paper:** Strogatz, Abrams, McRobie, Eckhardt & Ott, "Crowd synchrony on the Millennium Bridge", *Nature* 438, 43–44, 2005. [V citation and abstract excerpts; the author list and page numbers are from memory [M]]
- Key finding: the London Millennium Bridge wobbled sideways on opening day (June 2000) because walkers started to step in sync with the sway. Wobble and synchrony appear together once the crowd passes a critical size. Each walker speeds or slows their gait to match the sway, so the more they sway, the more in sync they are.
- Mechanism in plain words: a feedback loop. A small sway nudges walkers into rhythm, in-rhythm steps push harder on the bridge, a bigger sway nudges more walkers. Below a critical crowd size nothing happens; above it, the whole bridge goes. The remedy is more damping.
- Caveat [V]: a *Physics Today* letter disputes this, arguing the bridge's first lateral mode (about 0.5 Hz) is far from walking frequency (about 1 Hz), so the synchrony might not be the cause. Treat as "debated".

**Game skill:** *Slay the Spire*, Silent card **Catalyst**: doubles the Poison on an enemy; the upgraded version triples it [V, with the caveat that guides for StS 2 describe the upgrade differently]. (Poison deals its stack as damage at end of turn, then falls by 1 [V].) Also **Echo Form** (Defect): the first card played each turn is played twice [M]. Why it's fun: you spend turns piling small stacks so that one card finishes the job; the exponent comes from timing, not the card itself. A threshold payoff in the same family as the bridge.

## 2. melee | cellular automata | party game

**Paper:** Bak, Chen & Creutz, "Self-organized criticality in the 'Game of Life'", *Nature* 342, 780–782, 1989. [V: Nature vol. 342 and the finding that local configurations self-organise into a critical state; authors and page range from memory [M]]
- Key finding: starting from random soup, Life's patterns settle into a critical state where the sizes of "avalanches" of change follow a power law (small events common, huge ones rare but possible).
- Mechanism: each cell's fate depends only on the 8 neighbours (survive with 2–3, born with exactly 3); local rules, no central control, yet large-scale structure emerges.
- Background [V]: Life was introduced to the public in Martin Gardner's column in *Scientific American*, October 1970; Conway's aim was an interesting, unpredictable automaton. Gliders (a five-cell shape that moves diagonally) are a famous Life object [M].

**Game mechanic:** *Fall Guys*, **Hex-A-Gone** (and similar "Block Party/Tile" rounds): the floor is hexagonal tiles that disappear shortly after a player steps on them, so the arena's shape is rewritten by the players' footsteps and the layers drop away [M, not searched]. Why it's fun: the board is a shared, visibly shrinking resource, and everybody's choice changes everybody else's options.

## 3. melee | archer fish spitting | golf game

**Paper:** Schlegel & Schuster, "Small circuits for large tasks: high-speed decision-making in archerfish", *Science* 319, 104–106, 2008. [V title/journal/volume from a DFG listing; author order should be rechecked]
- Key finding [V from Schuster 2023 review]: when a shot insect falls, the fish picks a fast turn ("predictive C-start") using information gathered only briefly after the prey starts moving, and turns directly towards the later landing point. It does this faster than ordinary reaction time and arrives at the right point before the prey lands, with other fish racing to the same spot.
- Why it is hard [V]: neither the shooter nor a bystander knows where the prey will land because the jet's impact can send the prey in different directions and speeds.
- Extra [M]: a later study (Gerullis & Schuster 2014, *Current Biology*) reports that archerfish shape the speed of their jet during the shot so that the water merges into a single mass on arrival; I did not verify this. Archerfish are described as the only one of about 35,000 fish species able to shoot water at distant targets [V]. Schuster 2023 is a review in *J Comp Physiol A*, "The archerfish predictive C-start" [V].

**Game mechanic:** *Golf With Your Friends* power-ups in custom mode: Double Jump boosts your own shot, Honey Trap slows opponents, others include freezing players, turning an opponent's ball square, and (WORMS crossover) jet pack and snap freeze [V, no timings]. A second, classic mechanic: the Hot Shots / Everybody's Golf **three-click swing** (click for start, power, then accuracy/impact) [M]. Why it's fun: the shot is a timed three-beat performance, and a mistimed third click changes the ball's path.

## 4. melee | echolocation in bats | trading card game

**Paper:** Kalko & Schnitzler, Daubenton's bat echolocation and hunting behaviour (*Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology*, 1989) [? search found the journal series and the abstracts but not the exact authors/title; treat the citation as unverified]. The phase structure itself comes from Griffin, Webster & Michael, "The echolocation of flying insects by bats", *Animal Behaviour* 8, 141–154, 1960 [M].
- Key findings [V from abstracts]: hunting bats pass through search, approach and terminal (buzz) phases; call rate climbs from about 5–8 Hz in search to about 12–20 Hz in approach and then a very rapid buzz; in approach, call duration and pulse interval shrink and calls come in groups of increasing size. In Daubenton's bat the buzz has two parts; buzz I ended on average 47–63 ms before contact, about bat reaction time, so the final part is not used to steer. In *Pipistrellus kuhlii* a pause of about 100 ms after the buzz marked a successful capture.
- Mechanism in plain words: ping slowly while searching; as the target gets closer, ping faster and shorter because you need more updates per second and echoes return sooner.

**Game mechanic:** *Hearthstone* **Echo** (Witchwood): an Echo card can be played again in the same turn, as copies stay in hand until end of turn, so you can keep deploying until mana runs out [V]. I could not find a Hearthstone "echolocation" keyword [V negative]. Another possible match is **Discover** (choose one of three) [M, not searched]. Why it's fun: repetition and a hard end-of-turn limit (mana) turn into a risky chain, and the chain is readable.

## 5. ranged | sonoluminescence | grand strategy

**Paper:** Gaitan, Crum, Church & Roy, "Sonoluminescence and bubble dynamics for a single, stable, cavitation bubble", *J. Acoust. Soc. Am.* 91, 3166–3183, 1992. [V title/authors/year from search; page range [M]]
- Key finding [V]: a single gas bubble in glycerine–water driven at about 21–25 kHz pulsates strongly and emits a short flash of light once per acoustic cycle, apparently from the bubble's centre. The effect was first achieved in 1988 [V, secondary].
- Mechanism [V from Brenner, Hilgenfeldt & Lohse, *Rev. Mod. Phys.* 74, 425, 2002]: the leading explanation is adiabatic heating at the collapse, partially ionising the gas inside and giving thermal emission (e.g. bremsstrahlung); shock waves inside the bubble do not seem to play a prominent role. In plain words: a sound wave swells then crushes a bubble so hard that the gas inside becomes hot enough to glow.

**Game mechanic:** *Sid Meier's Civilization VI* **Eureka / Inspiration boosts**: doing a specific act (e.g. mining three times) grants a large part of the cost of the related tech or civic [M, not searched; I recall 50% for Eurekas]. Alternative: *Crusader Kings III* casus belli, where the claim type limits what the winner can take and long wars drain the realm [V community sources, not official]. Why Civ's Eureka is fun: a modest preparatory action is turned into a sudden, large payoff.

## 6. ranged | plant electrical signalling | extraction shooter

**Paper:** Böhm, Scherzer, Krol, Kreuzer, Larisch, Becker, Al-Rasheid, Federle & Hedrich, "The Venus flytrap *Dionaea muscipula* counts prey-induced action potentials to induce sodium uptake", *Current Biology* 26, 286–295, 2016. [M — the search did not return it; verify]
- Key finding [M]: the trap counts action potentials; two trigger-hair touches close the trap, and more action potentials (about five) switch on the digestive response and sodium uptake.
- Challenge [V]: Burri et al., "A single touch can provide sufficient mechanical stimulation to trigger Venus flytrap closure", *PLOS Biology*, July 2020 — a model and a force-sensing microrobot show one touch can sometimes produce two action potentials, so the "two touches within 30 s" rule is not absolute [V]. The accepted older rule: two touches within ~30 s [V]. Action-potential firing is not always reliable, depending on season, temperature and plant health [V, Backyard Brains].
- Plain words: the plant has a memory that decays; one touch is nothing, two in quick succession means a real insect.

**Game mechanic:** *Escape from Tarkov*: players moving in a very slow crouch ("crab-walking") became too quiet and therefore too effective, which was reported as a problem; suppressors reduce weapon sound and make location harder to pinpoint [V, secondary news]. *Hunt: Showdown* is praised for binaural audio that lets players pinpoint threats [V]. Hunt's crows that scatter when startled give away positions [M]. Why it's fun: noise is a currency; every action has a loudness cost that others can hear.

## 7. ranged | octopus camouflage | farming sim

**Paper:** Hanlon, Chiao, Mäthger, Barbosa, Buresch & Chubb, "Cephalopod dynamic camouflage: bridging the continuum between background matching and disruptive coloration", *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* 364, 429–437, 2009. [V partially: the paper (PMC2674088) and its three-pattern framework surfaced; authors and pages from memory [M]]
- Key findings [V]: patterns fall into uniform, mottle and disruptive types. Uniform and mottle serve background matching; disruptive patterns mainly enhance disruptiveness and also aid background matching. Mottle and disruptive patterns are often mixed. Size, contrast and edges of background objects are the key cues. Chromatophore control is by direct neural command, with over 30 million chromatophore organs per animal (per an NPR interview with Hanlon) [V].
- Surprising extra [M]: Ramirez & Oakley, *J Exp Biol* 2015, found octopus skin senses light by itself (LACE). Not verified.
- Plain words: the animal doesn't copy the background exactly; it picks one of a few "recipes" based on edges and contrast.

**Game mechanic:** *Stardew Valley* **sprinklers**: Basic waters the four adjacent tiles (+ shape), Quality the 3×3 ring (8 tiles), Iridium the 5×5 block (24 tiles) [V]. **Scarecrows** protect crops in a radius of about 8 tiles, but sources disagree on the shape and size (circle vs 16×16) [V, ?]. Why it's fun: placing area tools in a grid is a puzzle with a clear upgrade ladder.

## 8. ranged | mycorrhizal fungal networks | physics puzzle game

**Paper:** Simard, Perry, Jones, Myrold, Durall & Molina, "Net transfer of carbon between ectomycorrhizal tree species in the field", *Nature* 388, 579–582, 1997. [V citation (Mother Tree Project lists it as "Net transfer of carbon between tree species with shared ectomycorrhizal fungi"); my memory has "in the field" in the title — unresolved [?]]
- Key finding [V secondary]: seedlings of paper birch, Douglas fir, and western red cedar (the control, which does not share the fungi) were grown together. Net carbon flowed between birch and fir via shared fungi; secondary source says the net flow ran toward Douglas fir.
- Debate [V secondary]: a 2023 critique argues that net carbon transfer that benefits receiving seedlings is not sufficiently demonstrated (Karst et al., *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 2023 [M]). Gorzelak et al. 2015 (*AoB Plants* 7:plv050) is about inter-plant communication via the networks [V citation only].
- Plain words: trees tied to the same fungus swap sugar underground; whether that net-benefits the recipient is contested.

**Game mechanic:** *Portal 2* **gels**: blue gel makes surfaces bouncy, orange gel speeds up anything crossing it, white gel allows portals on otherwise blocked surfaces; portals are linked pairs and objects thrown into one exit from the other [V]. Why it's fun: you paint a surface once and its property then transforms every later object that touches it, so a network of painted points makes a path.

## 9. caster | Levy flight foraging | battle royale

**Paper:** Viswanathan, Buldyrev, Havlin, da Luz, Raposo & Stanley, "Optimizing the success of random searches", *Nature* 401, 911–914, 1999. [V title/journal/volume/page; author list [M]]
- Key finding [V secondary]: when targets are sparse and can be revisited, a search whose step lengths follow an inverse-square power law is optimal. This is a Lévy walk: mostly short steps with rare very long jumps.
- Follow-ups: Edwards et al., *Nature* 2007, reanalysed albatross, bumblebee and deer data and reported that the Lévy conclusions did not hold with more precise data [V secondary]. Humphries et al. 2010, "Environmental context explains Lévy and Brownian movement patterns of marine predators", *Nature* 465, 1066–1069 [title as given by the user's query, content unverified; I recall that predators switch between Lévy and Brownian depending on prey density [M]].
- Plain words: when food is rare, many small local searches punctuated by a few long hops beat both wandering randomly and always moving the same distance.

**Game mechanic:** the shrinking **storm/safe zone** in *Fortnite* and *PUBG* (and the ring in *Apex Legends*): the safe circle contracts in phases, the outside deals rising damage per tick, and the next circle is revealed shortly after the last closes [M, not searched]. Why it's fun: the map keeps forcing players inward; a rare long run (rotate) and many short moves decide who is where.

## 10. caster | slime mould network growth | hero shooter

**Paper:** Tero, Takagi, Saigusa, Ito, Bebber, Fricker, Yumiki, Kobayashi & Nakagaki, "Rules for biologically inspired adaptive network design", *Science* 327, 439–442, 22 January 2010. [V]
- Key finding [V]: the slime mould *Physarum polycephalum*, placed at "Tokyo" with food at the major cities of the region, grew a tube network comparable to the real Tokyo rail system in efficiency, fault tolerance and cost. The authors capture it with a mathematical model for network design.
- Mechanism in plain words [M]: tubes carrying more flow thicken, tubes carrying less flow thin and vanish, so the net starts dense and prunes itself to the useful routes.
- Earlier work [M]: Nakagaki et al. 2000, *Nature*, "Maze-solving by an amoeboid organism".

**Game mechanic:** *Overwatch* **Symmetra**: her primary beam locks onto a target within a short range and its damage ramps up through three levels while connected (about 2 seconds) [M, not searched, numbers unsure]; her Teleporter creates a linked pair of points [M]. ## 11. caster | cricket chirp and temperature | puzzle platformer

**Paper:** Dolbear, "The Cricket as a Thermometer", *The American Naturalist* 31, 970–971, 1897. [V year/title; journal volume and pages [M]]
- Key finding [V]: chirp rate predicts air temperature: T°F = 50 + (N60 − 40)/4, where N60 is chirps per minute; the simple version is chirps in 15 s plus 40 = °F. Dolbear did not name the species; the snowy tree cricket (*Oecanthus fultoni*) is the likely one. Margarette W. Brooks reported the relationship earlier, in 1881 [V]. Accuracy within a degree or two [V].
- Mechanism [V]: crickets are ectotherms; warmth speeds the muscle activity behind chirping.
- Extra [M]: Pires & Hoy, *J Comp Physiol A*, 1992, found that females' preference follows the male's temperature-shifted song, so both sender and receiver speed up together. Not verified.

**Game mechanic:** *Braid* world "Time and Place" (World 3): time moves forward when Tim moves right and backward when he moves left, so time is tied to position [M, not searched]. Alternative: *Celeste*'s single air dash, refilled on landing or by crystals [M]. Why it's fun: a puzzle whose only input is movement, where speed or direction of play is the key.

## 12. caster | ant colony foraging | tower defense

**Paper:** Deneubourg, Aron, Goss & Pasteels, "The self-organizing exploratory pattern of the Argentine ant", *Journal of Insect Behavior* 3, 159–168, 1990. [V content via secondary sources, citation details from memory [M]; one lecture note credits the modelling of the unequal-branch result to Goss et al. 1989 [V ?]]
- Key finding [V]: ants (*Linepithema humile*) between nest and food with a double bridge. With equal branches, small random differences are amplified until most ants use one branch. With unequal branches the shorter one wins, because ants that happen to choose it reach the food first and the return trip leaves more pheromone on it.
- Plain words: a trail is a vote; a positive feedback loop picks the route, and shorter routes get their votes sooner.
- A non-linear choice function (the "Deneubourg model") is still used to model binary choices [V].

**Game mechanic:** *Bloons TD 6* **Glue Gunner**: glue slows bloons and its upgrades add damage over time, corrosive glue, etc. [M, not searched]. Why it's fun: a slow zone multiplies the effect of every other tower on the path.

## 13. movement | tensegrity structures | stealth game

**Paper:** Ingber, "The Architecture of Life", *Scientific American* 278(1), 48–57, January 1998. [V]
- Key finding [V secondary]: cells keep their shape using tensegrity ("tensional integrity"), a balance of continuous tension and discontinuous compression, so internal prestress lets them resist outside forces and return to shape. Structures can be hierarchical (tensegrities within tensegrities). Later experiments reportedly support the idea at the scale of nuclei, cytoskeletal elements and single molecules, though it was initially controversial [V, secondary].
- Related primary paper [M]: Wang, Butler & Ingber, *Science* 260, 1124–1127, 1993, "Mechanotransduction across the cell surface and through the cytoskeleton".
- Plain words: a structure held up by tension in cables with a few struts floating in it; poke it and the whole thing shifts and springs back.

**Game mechanic:** *Dishonored* **Blink**: aim a short-range teleport, a placement marker shows where you will land, you arrive almost instantly and can pass through gaps and onto ledges; mana costs rise with upgrades like Dark Vision in the same toolset [M, not searched]. Why it's fun: instant relocation lets you choose your route through a guarded space.

## 14. defence | hummingbird torpor | city builder

**Paper:** Shankar, Hergenreder, Powers, et al. (authors partly from memory [M]), "Hummingbird torpor in context: duration, more than temperature, is the key to nighttime energy savings", *Journal of Avian Biology*, 2020. [V title/journal/year/findings]
- Key finding [V]: savings per hour in torpor were 65–92% versus normothermy and largest when air temperature neared a species' minimum body temperature; but total nighttime energy use depended most on how long the torpor bout lasted, which tracks night length, not on how much each hour saved. Data from Arizona and Ecuador.
- Related [V secondary]: Wolf et al., *Biology Letters* 2020, report that Andean hummingbirds can drop to about 3.3 °C (38 °F) at night (I did not retrieve the abstract). Minimum body temperature varies by species, 4–18 °C [V]. Older work (Hainsworth & Wolf 1970) says torpid hummingbirds become uncoordinated and insensitive to mild stimuli [V].
- Plain words: shut down almost everything overnight and the bird survives on its stored fuel; the longer it stays shut down, the more it saves.

## 15. utility | Chladni plates | immersive sim

**Paper:** Chladni, *Entdeckungen über die Theorie des Klanges* (Discoveries concerning the theory of sound), 1787 [V]. Faraday, "On a peculiar class of acoustical figures…", *Phil. Trans. R. Soc.* 121, 299–340, 1831 [M, not searched].
- Key finding [V]: sand on a bowed or driven plate collects on the nodal lines, the motionless parts, while the oscillating areas empty; each resonant frequency gives its own pattern. Hooke saw nodal patterns on vibrating glass plates in 1680. Liquid sits on the vibrating regions instead of the node lines (per Jenny, secondary) [V ?]. Sophie Germain won a prize with a fourth-order equation for plate vibration, later corrected for boundary conditions by Kirchhoff [V]. Faraday reported that fine light powders gather at the antinodes, because of air currents [M, not verified].
- Plain words: the frequency picks the pattern; the stuff lands where the plate is not moving. A 2024 preprint says understanding of the patterns remains incomplete [V].

**Game mechanic:** *Prey* (2017) **GLOO Cannon**: fires foam that hardens, freezing enemies and creating climbable platforms and plugs on walls, fires, and leaks [M, not searched]. Why it's fun: one tool, many uses; the player invents solutions in the level.

## 16. ultimate | magnetotactic bacteria | racing game

**Paper:** Blakemore, "Magnetotactic bacteria", *Science* 190, 377–379, 1975. [V year and the finding; volume/pages [M]]
- Key finding [V]: bacteria from sediments near Woods Hole swam consistently in one geographic direction and contained chains of iron-rich crystals, magnetosomes of magnetite (Fe3O4), arranged parallel to the swimming axis [V]. Earlier work by Salvatore Bellini in Italy (1950s–1963) described similar behaviour first [V].
- Mechanism [V]: the chain acts like a compass needle, aligning the cell along magnetic field lines. Magneto-aerotaxis: the bacteria use the field to swim along a line towards their preferred oxygen concentration; a marine coccus (strain MC-1) formed bands in capillary tubes at a preferred oxygen level (Frankel et al., *Biophysical Journal*, 1997) [V]. Some reverse direction under reducing conditions [V, Wikipedia mirror].
- Extra [M]: Blakemore, Frankel & Kalmijn (*Nature*, 1980) found that southern-hemisphere bacteria swim opposite to northern ones (both going "down" along the field). Not verified.
- Plain words: a tiny compass lines the cell up; it only needs to swim forward and the field points the way down to the right oxygen level.

**Game mechanic:** *Mario Kart* **drift mini-turbo**: hold drift through a turn, sparks change colour (blue, then orange, then pink/purple in later games), release for a speed boost proportional to the charge [M, not searched; spark colours differ between entries]. Why it's fun: a risky wide arc is rewarded with a boost, so turning becomes a charge-up.
